ABSTRACT
The forward modeling and parametric inversion of mechanical waves in physical systems are of significant 
importance in a multitude of scientific and engineering disciplines. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs), 
which have the potential to seamlessly integrate physical models and observational data, have emerged as an 
effective tool for wavefield modeling and inversion. Nevertheless, when solving for wavefields with multi- 
frequency and high-frequency characteristics, the inherent spectral bias and loss convergence imbalance of 
PINNs ultimately result in their inability to accurately model wave propagation. This paper proposes a new 
framework, the Physics-informed Fourier neural network (PIFNN), to address these issues by incorporating 
Fourier feature mapping and adaptive loss weights based on the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory. The 
robustness and effectiveness of the PIFNN method are demonstrated by improved accuracy in simulating wave 
propagation in numerical experiment. Furthermore, the training dynamics of wave propagation simulated by 
PIFNN are analyzed through the lens of NTK to enhance interpretability.
1. Introduction
The forward simulation of the mechanical wave in physical systems, 
coupled with the inverse identification of their parameters, represents a 
significant area of research, with applications in various fields, including 
non-destructive testing ( Yin and Shi, 2022 ), medical imaging ( Yin et al., 
2024 ), metamaterial structural design ( Cho et al., 2024 ), seismology 
( Ding et al., 2023 ), electromagnetic ( Jeong et al., 2024 ) and ocean sonar 
detection ( Ren et al., 2022 ). Ultrasound, as a type of mechanical wave, is 
used as an effective physical sensing method, and its propagation 
properties in a medium including scattering, reflection and refraction 
can be analyzed to characterize unknown material properties and ge -
ometries ( Wu et al., 2023a ; Xu et al., 2024 ). In recent decades, numerous 
numerical methods have been developed to solve partial differential 
equations (PDEs) describing wave propagation, thereby facilitating 
detailed analyses of mechanical wave in diverse physical systems, 
including finite element method (FEM) ( Li et al., 2024 ), finite difference 
time domain method (FDTD) ( Shivaprasad et al., 2018 ), and boundary 
element method (BEM) ( Shieh et al., 2018 ). Nevertheless, predictive 
modeling of complex spatiotemporal dynamics using traditional nu -
merical methods can pose significant challenges in many practical 
scenarios: (1) The computational paradigms employed are based on 
mesh partitioning, necessitating the use of finer meshes and time steps 
for simulating ultrasound propagation in complex materials, thereby 
increasing the demand for computational resources (2) Numerical sim -
ulations are usually time-consuming, rendering them impractical for 
many applications that require repeated forward modeling, such as in -
verse problems, optimization design, and uncertainty quantification. (3) 
Traditional numerical methods are limited to integrating multi-physics 
phenomena and deriving equivalent simplified mathematical formula -
tions for ease of implementation. While some methods have been 
developed to alleviate these issues, more cost-effective solutions are still 
needed ( Gu and Jing, 2018 ).
Recently, researchers have begun to explore the application of deep 
neural network methods to overcome the above limitations, prompting 
further work on numerical simulation of ultrasound, leading to a deeper 
understanding of propagation mechanisms and advances in ultrasound 
imaging techniques. Alguacil et al. constructed a convolutional neural 
network with multi-scale capabilities, using data from Lattice- 
Boltzmann to accelerate the acoustic wave propagation simulation in a 
two-dimensional quiescent medium ( Alguacil et al., 2021 ). Gantal used 
simulation data to train deep neural networks to learn elastic wave

More importantly, in larger solution domains, more complex excitation

Despite advancements, deep neural networks remain heavily 
dependent on sparse data and often lack physical interpretability, 
limiting their applicability in many science and engineering fields. Re -
searchers have proposed integrating physical laws with data-driven 
concepts to address this limitation, paving a new paradigm for scienti -
fic discovery and translation ( Karniadakis et al., 2021 ). Within this 
physics-enhanced machine learning framework, different types of biases 
can guide learning and improve generalization even with limited data 
( Cicirello, 2024 ). Observational bias leverages informative data 
reflecting the underlying physics, while learning bias shapes the training 
process through model design and optimization strategies. Inductive 
bias imposes prior assumptions or physical constraints such as conser -
vation laws and boundary conditions, and model form bias incorporates 
partial knowledge of the governing equations to constrain the solution 
space. Physics-informed neural networks (PINNs) are a representative 
example, where inductive and model form biases are embedded in the 
loss function via automatic differentiation ( Paszke et al., 2017 ), 
demonstrating significant potential for solving partial differential 
equations (PDEs) and inverse problems, allowing diverse types of 
boundary conditions (BCs) and initial conditions (ICs) ( Raissi et al., 
2019 ). Karniadakis presented a first-time unified framework applicable 
to wave propagation and full waveform inversion that predicts the 
displacement field over the entire computational domain using only two 
time slices of wavefield data ( Rasht-Behesht et al., 2022 ). Liu et al. 
applied PINNs to the numerical simulation of transcranial ultrasound, 
illustrating PINNs' scalability without the need for expensive solutions 
( Wang et al., 2023 ). Thakur proposed a neural green-distributed point 
source method for modeling ultrasonic field emission near a fluid-solid 
interface, which utilizes PINNs to evaluate the Green's function and its 
gradient, thereby addressing the shortcomings of traditional numerical 
methods ( Thakur et al., 2024 ). Schmid demonstrates that PINN can 
accurately predict the sound pressure field and estimate the boundary 
conductance well, even when dealing with sparse and noisy experi -
mental sound pressure data ( Schmid et al., 2024 ). Moreover, migration 
learning enables PINNs to address diverse acoustic scattering issues and 
predict pertinent physical quantities using trained models, whereas 
conventional numerical techniques necessitate the reconstruction of the 
simulation ( Veerababu and Ghosh, 2024 ). In contrast to conventional 
neural networks, PINNs utilize physical laws to provide prior knowl -
edge, thereby markedly reducing the reliance on observational data 
while exhibiting accelerated convergence and superior generalization 
capabilities.
However, PINNs train pathologically or even fail completely on some 
problems, especially since the PDE solutions include high-frequency and 
multi-frequency features. Recent studies have revealed two fundamental 
weaknesses in PINNs ( Wang et al., 2021 ). The first is related to the 
intrinsic property present in neural networks, which cannot learn 
high-frequency functions well, leading to inaccurate predictions, called 
spectral bias ( Rahaman et al., 2019 ). Wang et al. employ the recently 
developed Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory to confirm that PINN 
does indeed exhibit the phenomenon of spectral bias, which is the main 
reason that hinders the learning of high-frequency components ( Wang 
et al., 2022 ). This presents a significant challenge for the multiscale 
modeling of ultrasound. This paper reveals this phenomenon in the 
context of a one-dimensional wave equation. The second fundamental 
weakness of PINNs is transforming the forward problem into an un -
constrained optimization problem with multiple loss functions, which 
encounters an order of magnitude difference between the different terms 
of the loss function ( Wang et al., 2022 ). Consequently, an imbalance in 
gradient flow dynamics of the different terms of the composite loss 
function during training progress results in the inability to obtain the 
correct solution, indicating that the PDE residual terms with scattering 
information and the BC terms with reflection information can 'fail' 
rendering the standard PINN unable to consistently and accurately 
capture the reflection and scattering features in transient mechanical 
wave propagation.
In light of the aforementioned analysis, one potential avenue for 
addressing the training pathology is to empirically provide an 'infor -
mation encoding' that mitigates the difficulty of fitting known high 
frequencies. Tancik et al. introduced a Fourier feature network that 
employs a straightforward mapping of Fourier features to enhance the 
capacity of a fully connected network to learn high-frequency functions 
( Tancik et al., 2020 ). Jagtap et al. proposed the PINN with an adaptive 
activation function (aPINN), which is capable of continuously capturing 
the diverse frequency bands present in the solution ( Jagtap et al., 2020 ). 
Another way to alleviate the training pathology is to balance the 
multitasking loss function through a weight adjustment strategy. 
McClenny proposed a soft attention mechanism approach that can solve 
complex problems more efficiently by subdividing the weights of each 
configuration point ( McClenny and Braga-Neto, 2020 ). The empirical 
weight strategies limit the optimization of arbitrary ICs and BCs. 
Therefore, developing a dynamic adjustment strategy based on different 
terms of the loss function gradient flow is necessary. Xiang et al. pro -
posed a method based on maximum likelihood estimation to set the 
weights of different losses, improving prediction accuracy ( McClenny 
and Braga-Neto, 2020 ). In addition, wang et al. proposed a new opti -
mization method to adaptively balance the convergence speed based on 
the law that neural networks converge along different decomposition 
directions of the eigenfunctions of the NTK ( Wang et al., 2018 ). In 
particular, Lu et al. proposed a PINN method with hard constraints 
(hPINNs), incorporating strictly ICs and BCs into the neural network 
architecture by modifying the network output, eliminating the imbal -
ance in gradient flow dynamics ( Lu et al., 2021 ). Overall, while these 
improvements, including modifications to activation functions 
( Al-Safwan et al., 2021 ; Huang and Alkhalifah, 2022 ), enhancements to 
input representations ( Huang and Alkhalifah, 2024 ), and refinements to 
incorporating boundary conditions ( Alkhadhr and Almekkawy, 2023 ), 
have enhanced the ability of PINNs to capture high-frequency wavefield 
solutions. intrinsic spectral bias and loss convergence imbalance 
continue to limit accurate learning of high-frequency components, 
suggesting that further methodological developments are needed to 
fully address these training pathologies.
The significant challenges associated with PINNs in modelling wave 
propagation problems are the spectral bias, which hinders accurate 
learning of high frequency components critical for scattering and 
reflection, and the loss convergence imbalance, which prevents 
boundary and initial conditions from being satisfied accurately, thereby 
obstructing convergence to the exact solution. This study addresses the 
training pathology of PINNs in modeling wave propagation and provides 
a reliable solution to the fundamental problem in time-domain ultra -
sonic wave modeling. In particular, it investigates the applicability and 
generality of PINNs in capturing reflections and scattering generated by 
wave interaction with complex medium. Specifically, it focuses on the 
accurate detection of reflection effects induced by interlayer medium 
using Fourier feature PINNs, the fast convergence using an adaptive 
weighting strategy, and the study of the relationship between the 
training dynamics of the neural network and the accuracy in predicting 
wave propagation features. This fundamental work can be further 
extended to modeling wave propagation in complex medium with 
arbitrary velocity distributions. The main contributions and implica -
tions of the current work are summarized as follows: 
• A novel framework, PIFNN, has been devised to address wave 
propagation issues. PIFNN integrates a Fourier feature layer and an 
adaptive loss weighting method based on NTK theory, thereby 
addressing the challenge of training pathologies arising from spectral

2.3. Physics-informed Fourier neural network (PIFNN)
The wave propagation in inhomogeneous medium is a highly com -
plex physical process, and its scattering process causes wave distortion 
and mixing, which is manifested as the superposition of high and low 
frequencies on different spatial and temporal scales ( G ¨ortz et al., 2023 ). 
However, the spectral bias indicates that neural networks tend to learn 
low-frequency features during training, with deficiencies in learning 
multi-frequency or high-frequency features. The spectral bias of PINNs is 
much more severe due to the additional physics constraints, resulting in 
difficulties for PINNs in accurately capturing reflection and scattering 
features in simulated wave propagation. To mitigate the pathology of 
spectral bias, this subsection introduces a stochastic Fourier feature 
mapping of the coordinates of the training points to enhance the capa -
bility of PINNs in learning high-frequency features.

2.4. Adaptive loss weight training algorithm for PINNs/PIFNNs
The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) theory elucidates that the eigen -
values of the NTK eigenfunctions in different directions determine the 
rate of convergence of the deep neural network along the corresponding

3. Results and discussion
In this section, the proposed method is validated through numerical 
experiments; exploring the method on a simple string vibration problem 
is a prerequisite for extending it to complex problems. Then, the pro -
posed method is evaluated in a two-dimensional inhomogeneous me -
dium to assess its predictive ability for ultrasonic propagation. In the 
tests, the numerical solutions obtained by FDTD are considered as the 
reference solutions to evaluate the effectiveness and accuracy of the 
prediction results of PIFNN. The proposed PIFNN is compared with the 
traditional PINN methods. To fairly compare the performance of the 
different models, the same hidden layer parameters and training points 
are used for PIFNN and PINN. The neural network approximates the 
solution of the PDE by minimizing a composite loss function to predict 
the wavefield at any time and location, requiring two early-time snap -
shots as training data. Undeniably, the training data for the initial 
conditions set by the network are derived from sampling the wavefield 
data obtained from analytical formulations or finite differences, which 
are intended to constitute a complete initial marginal problem. It is 
worth noting that other data-driven methods may not accurately predict 
ultrasonic propagation with wavefields using only two early time 
snapshots, demonstrating the superiority of the PIFNN method for 
solving the wave equation with a small amount of data. Due to the non- 
convex nature of the optimization problem, it is not possible to find a 
unique solution. It follows that the neural network's hyperparameters 
can impact the training results, and the hyperparameters for all exper -
iments were obtained after tuning. All numerical experiments were 
conducted with the Adam ( Kingma and Ba, 2014 ) during optimization 
were coded in Pytorch 1.13 ( Paszke et al., 2019 ) with Python 3.9. These 
numerical experiments were performed on a computer with an NVIDIA 
RTX A6000 card (48 GB) and an Intel 4210R central processing unit 
(CPU). It is worth noting that Appendix A provides the detailed 
configuration of the networks used in the experiment. The accuracy of

PIFNN, designed to address the challenge of wave propagation. It in -
tegrates the Fourier feature layer and the adaptive loss weight method 
based on the NTK theory, thereby addressing the training pathologies 
caused by the dynamics of spectral differences and imbalance loss 
gradients.
